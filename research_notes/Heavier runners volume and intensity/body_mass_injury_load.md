# Body Mass / BMI, Running Volume, Intensity and Running-Related Injury (RRI) Risk

Method note: These notes come from web search result abstracts and snippets. Full-text fetches failed because pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and blogs.bmj.com are not reachable from this session's network. Every number below is copied from an abstract, registry or press summary. Numbers that need checking against the full text are flagged. Evidence grades are my own judgement (High / Moderate / Low / Very low), roughly GRADE-style.

## Q1. What do the key studies find on BMI or body mass as an RRI risk factor?

### Takeaway
In novice runners, BMI >30 is a fairly consistent but modest predictor of RRI. The evidence is moderate and comes from DANO-RUN, GRONORUN and the 2024 Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort. In recreational and experienced runners, body mass is often not linked to injury: in the Malisoux 2020 RCT the subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR) was 1.00 per kg. Systematic reviews rank BMI below previous injury as a risk factor. So the risk from body mass looks concentrated in novices, and especially in people with BMI >30.

### Cited Findings
- **DANO-RUN predictors (Nielsen et al., Orthop J Sports Med 2013;1(1):2325967113487316)**
  - Design: 1-year prospective cohort of 930 novice runners, all given neutral shoes. 254 sustained an RRI over 155,318 km of running.
  - Factors linked to higher injury risk: BMI >30 kg/m², age 45–65, noncompetitive behaviour, and previous non-running injury.
  - BMI <20 was reported as protective.
  - The overall chi-square test across four BMI groups was only borderline (P = .06).
  - Source: [dissem.in record](https://dissem.in/p/816182/predictors-of-running-related-injuries-among-930-novice-runners); [ortopaedi.dk abstract](https://ortopaedi.dk/fileadmin/abstracts/2012/files/assets/basic-html/page60.html)
- **DANO-RUN pronation paper (Nielsen et al., BJSM 2014;48(6):440-447)**
  - 927 novice runners (1,854 feet).
  - BMI was an adjustment covariate here, not the exposure. This is the "neutral shoe" paper, not a BMI paper. Do not cite it as "BMI >30 not more injured."
  - Source: [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/6/440.short); [dissem.in](https://dissem.in/p/4005271/foot-pronation-is-not-associated-with-increased-injury-risk-in-novice-runners-wearing-a-neutral-shoe-a-1-year-prospective-cohort-study)
- **GRONORUN (Buist et al., Am J Sports Med 2010;38(2):273-280)**
  - Design: prospective cohort of 532 novice runners in a 13-week program for a 4-mile race. 21% sustained an RRI.
  - In men, higher BMI, previous injury and earlier sport without axial loading predicted RRI. No clear predictors were found for women.
  - Effect sizes were not available in the snippets.
  - Source: [PubMed 19966104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19966104/); [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/19966104/predictors-of-running-related-injuries-in-novice-runners-enrolled-in-a-systematic-training-program-a-prospective-cohort-study)
- **Buist & Bredeweg, "Higher risk of injury in overweight novice runners" (BJSM 2011)**
  - Combined GRONORUN data: 25% (82/334) of overweight novices sustained an RRI, compared with 15% (76/514) of the lower-BMI group.
  - Source: [BJSM 45(4):338](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/45/4/338.1) (numbers as summarised in search results; verify the exact BMI cut-off, probably BMI ≥25)
- **Garmin-RUNSAFE 18-month cohort (Eur J Sport Sci 2024, DOI 10.1002/ejsc.12206)**
  - Runners with high BMI plus a running-related problem in the past 3 months form a "high-risk population."
  - A search snippet says those with BMI >30 and a previous problem had the highest injury risk, at 71%. The full text was not retrieved, so verify the 71% and its time horizon.
  - Population: recreational runners using Garmin devices, not only novices.
  - Source: [PMC11680559](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11680559/)
- **Malisoux et al., Am J Sports Med 2020 (RCT)**
  - 848 recreational runners, 6-month follow-up.
  - Body mass was not linked to injury: SHR 1.00 per kg (95% CI 0.99–1.01).
  - Source: [ClinicalTrials NCT03115437](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03115437); [LIH portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/shoe-cushioning-influences-the-running-injury-risk-according-to-b/)
- **Theisen et al., BJSM 2014 (RCT)**
  - 247 runners.
  - Midsole hardness was not linked to injury, but BMI was positively associated with injury risk.
  - Source: as described in the [Malisoux search summary/LIH](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/shoe-cushioning-body-mass-and-running-biomechanics-as-risk-factor/) (secondary description; verify)
- **Saragiotto et al., Sports Med 2014;44(8):1153-63**
  - Systematic review of 11 prospective cohorts.
  - The main risk factor was previous injury in the last 12 months.
  - The authors note that the small number of prospective studies limits their ability to detect risk factors.
  - Whether BMI reached significance was not retrievable.
  - Source: [PubMed 24809248](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24809248/); DOI 10.1007/s40279-014-0194-6
- **van Gent et al., BJSM 2007**
  - Lower-extremity RRI incidence ranged from 19.4% to 79.3%.
  - Strong evidence for two risk factors: long weekly distance in men and previous injury.
  - BMI was not highlighted in the abstract.
  - Source: [BJSM full PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/early/2007/05/01/bjsm.2006.033548.full.pdf)
- **Review of short- vs long-distance runners (PMC4338213, Sports Med 2015)**
  - In short-distance runners, higher BMI was a "strong" risk factor (moderate-quality evidence), alongside higher age, male sex, no previous running experience and lower running volume.
  - Source: [PMC4338213](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4338213)
- **Umbrella review of RRI risk factors**
  - The included reviews are of low quality and heterogeneous, so individual risk-factor findings need cautious interpretation.
  - Source: [umbrella review record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4090339?lng=en)
- **Videbæk et al., Sports Med 2015 (meta-analysis of 13 studies)**
  - Injury incidence was 17.8 per 1000 h in novice runners (95% CI 16.7–19.1), against 7.7 per 1000 h in recreational runners (95% CI 6.9–8.7).
  - This gives the novice-specific context: novices have about 2.3 times the hourly risk regardless of BMI.
  - Source: [PMC4473093](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4473093/); [PubMed 25951917](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25951917/)

### Inferences
- Grade for "BMI >30 increases RRI risk in novice runners": **Moderate**. Three independent cohorts point the same way (DANO-RUN, GRONORUN and Garmin-RUNSAFE), but some tests are borderline and the data are observational and partly self-reported.
- Grade for "higher body mass increases RRI risk in established recreational runners": **Low**, and the evidence conflicts. Malisoux 2020 found a null result per kg, while Theisen 2014 found a positive association.
- Heavier novices carry two risks at once: the high hourly novice incidence (17.8 per 1000 h) and the BMI effect. Previous injury interacts with high BMI, as in Garmin-RUNSAFE 2024.
- On the brief's mention of "BMI >30 not more injured?": I found no source showing that BMI >30 novices are *not* at higher risk. The DANO-RUN papers on BMI report higher risk or a distance-dependent risk.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes (HR/OR) for BMI in Buist 2010 AJSM and Nielsen 2013 OJSM were not available from the snippets.
- I could not confirm a "Kemler meta-analysis" on BMI and RRI. Searches found none, so it may not exist or may be named differently. The Kemler/Dutch work I am aware of could not be verified.
- No study used a strict >90 kg cut-off. Studies use BMI >30, BMI ≥25 or the median mass (Malisoux: 78.2 kg for men, 62.8 kg for women).
- I could not retrieve whether Saragiotto 2014 found BMI significant.

## Q2. Do heavier runners tolerate less volume or less rapid progression? Is there a BMI × pace/intensity interaction?

### Takeaway
The only direct evidence of an interaction between BMI and volume comes from DANO-RUN.
- **Cohort finding:** Obese novices who ran ≥3 km in their first week had more injuries.
- **RCT test:** A small randomised trial (n=56) tested this. The intention-to-treat result was not significant (RD −16.3%), but the per-protocol results favoured starting at 3 km.

For progression of volume or pace in general, the evidence is low:
- The >30% rule comes from one cohort with HR 1.59 that was not significant.
- Run Clever found no difference between progressing intensity and progressing volume.
- No study tested whether BMI modifies the effect of pace or intensity.

### Cited Findings
- **Nielsen et al., Int J Sports Phys Ther 2014;9(3):338-345 (DANO-RUN secondary analysis)**
  - 933 novices; 56 injured during the first 3 weeks.
  - Runners with BMI >30 who ran 3–6 km or >6 km in week 1 had significantly more injuries than the reference group.
  - The authors suggest obese novices run <3 km in their first week.
  - Source: [pubmed.gov 24944852](https://www.pubmed.gov/24944852/); [PMC4060311](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060311)
  - The exact risk differences were not retrieved; verify them before citing.
- **Bertelsen, Rasmussen, Hansen, Nielsen, Int J Sports Phys Ther 2018;13(6):943-955 (RCT, NCT02014987)**
  - 56 obese novice runners randomised to start at 3 km per week (n=29) or 6 km per week (n=27). Both groups then added 10% per week for 4 weeks.
  - Intention-to-treat cumulative risk difference: −16.3% (95% CI −43.8% to 11.3%, p=0.25).
  - Per-protocol result for overuse-injury symptoms: RD −47.8% (95% CI −81.0% to −14.6%, p=0.01).
  - The Aarhus University news item reports a different per-protocol figure: −31.2% (95% CI −57.0% to −5.2%, p=0.02). The sources disagree on which per-protocol number to report.
  - Adherence was poor: 15 participants uploaded no data and 5 ran far more than prescribed.
  - Sources: [RUNSAFE news](https://runsafe.au.dk/display/artikel/obese-novice-runners-should-consider-their-start-to-run-distance-carefully); [ClinicalTrials NCT02014987](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02014987); [PubMed 30534460](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30534460/)
- **Nielsen et al., JOSPT 2014 ("Excessive progression in weekly running distance")**
  - 874 novices with GPS watches, followed for 1 year.
  - Progressing weekly distance by >30% (vs <10%) over 2 weeks gave HR 1.59 (95% CI 0.96–2.66; P=.07).
  - Associations were stronger for "distance-related" injuries such as patellofemoral pain and ITB syndrome.
  - The authors call the study explorative.
  - BMI was not reported as an effect modifier.
  - Source: [AAU VBN record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/excessive-progression-in-weekly-running-distance-and-risk-of-runn); [JOSPT summary PDF](https://www.orthobethesda.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/jospt-running-increased-mileage.pdf)
- **Run Clever (Ramskov et al., BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med 2018;4:e000333; companion paper in JOSPT 2018)**
  - 839 recreational runners started an 8-week shared preconditioning period. 447 then entered a 16-week period with either an intensity-progression schedule (Sch-I) or a volume-progression schedule (Sch-V).
  - 80 runners were injured (Sch-I 36, Sch-V 44).
  - 16-week risk difference: −14.0% (95% CI −36.9% to 8.9%). All CIs crossed zero.
  - Sources: [BMJ OSEM abstract](https://bmjopensem.bmj.com/content/4/1/e000333.abstract); [full PDF](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/files/284775170/e000333.full.pdf); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/53731)
- **Run Clever secondary analysis of combined pace and volume change**
  - A secondary analysis reportedly found a significant positive interaction between pace change and volume change. Changing both at once may increase injury risk beyond what each adds alone.
  - Details, the year and any BMI stratification were not retrieved.
  - Source: as summarised in search results for [Run Clever/AAU](https://vbn.aau.dk/en/publications/run-clever-no-difference-in-risk-of-injury-when-comparing-progres) (secondary description; verify)

### Inferences
- Grade for "obese (BMI >30) novices should start with a low initial volume (<3 km in week 1)": **Low to moderate**. It is supported by one cohort plus an underpowered RCT that was non-significant by intention to treat but positive per protocol. This is the strongest BMI × volume evidence available.
- Grade for "a >30% weekly increase raises injury risk": **Low**. The HR of 1.59 is not significant.
- Grade for "BMI modifies tolerance to progression of volume or pace": **Very low**. I found no study that tested it directly.
- Grade for "intensity progression vs volume progression": **Low to moderate** for no difference in recreational runners, from Run Clever, which was not stratified by mass.
- Practical inference: heavier novices may benefit from low starting volume and from not increasing pace and volume at the same time. The second point extrapolates from the Run Clever interaction, not from BMI-specific data.

### Gaps
- No study used Ramskov-type pace progression stratified by BMI.
- The exact risk differences in the Nielsen 2014 IJSPT paper were not retrieved.
- I found no data on whether heavier runners need a smaller weekly % increase once they are past the novice phase.

## Q3. Mass and mechanical loading: bone stress, tendon and joint load, and footwear or cadence for heavier runners

### Takeaway
Extra mass raises absolute ground reaction forces and joint and bone loads. However:
- GRF metrics correlate poorly with tibial bone load.
- Direct per-km tissue-load data stratified by body mass are scarce.

Footwear evidence goes against intuition. In the Malisoux 2020 RCT, softer cushioning protected *lighter* runners but made no difference for heavier runners. I found no trial evidence on cadence specific to heavier runners.

### Cited Findings
- **Malisoux et al., Am J Sports Med 2020 (RCT, 848 runners)**
  - Hard vs soft shoe, all runners: SHR 1.52 (95% CI 1.07–2.16).
  - Lighter runners (below the median mass: 78.2 kg for men, 62.8 kg for women): SHR 1.80 (95% CI 1.09–2.98).
  - Heavier runners: SHR 1.23 (95% CI 0.75–2.03).
  - Midsole stiffness was 61.3 N/mm (soft) vs 94.9 N/mm (hard).
  - The authors' hypothesis that heavier runners would be more injured and benefit most from cushioning was not supported.
  - Sources: [LIH portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/shoe-cushioning-influences-the-running-injury-risk-according-to-b/); [NCT03115437](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03115437); protocol: [BMJ Open 2017;7:e017379](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/8/e017379.abstract)
  - A new trial (NCT06384872) is recruiting to test cushioning position by body mass: [record](https://decentrialz.com/clinical-trials/effect-shoe-cushioning-position-properties-running-06384872)
- **Matijevich et al., PLoS ONE 2019**
  - Increases in GRF metrics were not strongly correlated with increases in tibial compression force across speeds and slopes.
  - The authors caution against using GRF as a proxy for bone load or injury risk.
  - Source: [DOAJ PLoS ONE 2019](https://doaj.org/article/972aeed0eaad4f7d8b931462091921bc)
- **Load-carriage study, PubMed 32058282 (2020)**
  - Women running with 0–22.7 kg of added load.
  - Loads of ≥11.3 kg increased biomechanical variables associated with a history of tibial stress fracture.
  - Lighter participants changed their stride frequency more than heavier participants.
  - This uses added external load as a model, not habitual body mass.
  - Source: [PubMed 32058282](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058282/)
- **Finite-element study of women runners**
  - Peak tibial stress during running was more than 3 times that during walking.
  - Carrying 30% of body weight raised hip joint reaction force by about 49% of body weight.
  - Source: [DTIC report](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1034447.pdf)
- **Medial tibial stress syndrome reviews**
  - Weight is listed among the risk factors, alongside sex, age, bone density, previous injury and foot structure.
  - Source: [Springer review (BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2017)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1186/s12891-017-1481-9) (as summarised; verify)

### Inferences
- Grade for "higher mass means higher absolute load per step and per km": **High in principle**, because force scales with mass. But the translation to tissue injury is uncertain: bone and tendon adapt, and GRF is not tibial load.
- Grade for "heavier runners benefit more from cushioned shoes": **Low, and contradicted**. The best RCT (Malisoux 2020) shows the benefit was limited to lighter runners.
- Possible reason the BMI effect concentrates in novices: their tissues have not adapted to the higher absolute loads. This is speculative and fits the DANO-RUN week-1 finding.

### Gaps
- I found no prospective study of bone stress injury or tendinopathy incidence stratified by runner body mass (>90 kg).
- I found no RCT or cohort on cadence manipulation specific to heavier runners.
- I found no per-km cumulative tibial or Achilles load data across BMI categories in this search.

## Q4. Acute:chronic workload and single-session spikes: does body mass modify them?

### Takeaway
A large GPS cohort found that single-session distance spikes predict overuse RRI, while week-to-week change does not.
- Population and timing: Frandsen, Hulme, Nielsen et al., BJSM 2025; 5,205 runners followed for 18 months.
- I found nothing showing that body mass modifies the spike effect.

### Cited Findings
- **Frandsen, Hulme, Nielsen et al., BJSM 2025;59:1203-1210 (Garmin-RUNSAFE)**
  - 5,205 runners from 87 countries, 18-month follow-up.
  - Each session's distance was compared with the longest run in the previous 30 days.
  - Hazard ratios by spike size, as reported in search summaries:
    - Small spike (10–30%): HR 1.64
    - Moderate spike (30–100%): HR 1.52
    - Large spike (>100%): HR 2.28
  - Week-to-week (ACWR-style) change was not significantly associated with injury.
  - The authors conclude that overuse RRIs can arise suddenly within a single session.
  - Unresolved discrepancies:
    - The CIs were not retrieved.
    - A press quote ("up to 60% more likely" for 10–100% spikes) roughly matches the small and moderate HRs.
  - Sources: [PMC12421110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110); [Aarhus/LIH portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/how-much-running-is-too-much-identifying-high-risk-running-sessio/); [BJSM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bjsm/?p=11751); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1090184); [Outside](https://run.outsideonline.com/training/injuries-and-prevention/a-single-run-could-put-you-at-risk-for-an-overuse-injury-a-new-study-suggests/?scope=anon)
- **Same Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort, Eur J Sport Sci 2024 (DOI 10.1002/ejsc.12206)**
  - High BMI plus a recent running problem marks a high-risk subgroup.
  - The cohort's BMI paper and spike paper appear to be separate analyses; I found no combined spike × BMI analysis.
  - Source: [PMC11680559](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11680559/)

### Inferences
- Grade for "single-session spikes >10% above the 30-day longest run raise RRI risk": **Moderate**. The cohort is large, prospective and GPS-measured, but observational, and the HRs do not follow a monotonic dose-response.
- Grade for "body mass modifies spike risk": **No evidence found** (unknown). One plausible hypothesis, untested: heavier runners take on more absolute load in a spike, so a "longest run" cap may matter more for them. This should be stated as a hypothesis only.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether the 2025 spike paper adjusted for BMI or stratified by it, because the full text was blocked. This is worth checking in PMC12421110.
- The CIs for the spike HRs were not retrieved.
- I found no ACWR study with BMI interaction terms.

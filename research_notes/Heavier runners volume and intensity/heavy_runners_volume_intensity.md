# Weekly running volume and training intensity in heavier runners (>90 kg, high BMI, "Clydesdale", overweight recreational runners)

Note on method: these notes come from ~15 web searches. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov was not reachable from this session (DNS blocked), so many findings come from abstracts and search-result summaries rather than full texts. Effect sizes quoted here are from abstracts. Treat them as provisional.

Evidence grading used below: **Direct** = studies heavier/overweight runners and measures running volume or intensity against an outcome. **Partial** = studies the population or the variable, but not both together. **Extrapolation** = physiology or non-running populations used to make inferences.

**Headline count:**
- **Direct studies on volume in heavier runners: about 4, from a single Danish group (Nielsen/Bertelsen, Aarhus).** All of them measure **injury**, not performance or fitness.
- **Direct studies on intensity (intensity distribution, HIIT vs continuous, pace) in heavier *runners*, with performance or fitness outcomes: none found.**
- **Clydesdale-specific studies: none found.**
- Everything else is partial evidence or extrapolation. That includes HIIT vs MICT meta-analyses in overweight/obese non-athletes (mostly mixed modes, often cycling), cross-sectional predictor studies of recreational marathoners, and physiology/biomechanics of body mass.

## Q1. Are there studies that stratify runners by body mass/BMI and examine weekly mileage/volume or intensity vs performance or fitness outcomes?

### Takeaway
No study was found that stratifies runners by BMI or body mass and tests whether weekly volume or intensity distribution predicts **performance or fitness gains**. The only BMI-stratified volume studies are Danish novice-runner cohorts and one small RCT, and their outcome is **injury**. They suggest obese novices (BMI >30) should start below ~3 km/week. Cross-sectional studies of recreational racers include BMI/body fat and weekly volume as *co-predictors* of race time, but they do not analyse heavy runners as a subgroup.

### Cited Findings
**Direct (volume × BMI, injury outcome):**
- **Nielsen et al. 2014, prospective cohort, n=749 novice runners, 3 weeks (PMID 24944852).** Runners were grouped by BMI (≤30 vs >30) and by first-week distance. Obese runners had a significantly greater chance of injury if they ran 3–6 km or >6 km in week 1, compared with the low-distance, low-BMI reference group. The authors suggest that people with BMI >30 start with <3 km in week 1. The design is observational, so it cannot show causation. Grade: direct, low–moderate quality. — [PubMed 24944852](https://www.pubmed.gov/24944852/); [PMC4060311](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060311)
- **Bertelsen et al. 2018, RCT, n=56 obese novice runners (BMI 30–35), 4 weeks (PMID 30534460).** The trial compared a start of 3 km/week (n=29) with 6 km/week (n=27). The intention-to-treat result was not significant: cumulative injury risk difference −16.3% (95% CI −43.8% to 11.3%; p=0.25). The per-protocol result was significant: −31.2% (95% CI −57.0% to −5.2%; p=0.02). This is the only randomized volume trial found in heavier runners. It is underpowered and short, and its only outcome is injury. Grade: direct, low quality (small, ITT null). — [PubMed 30534460](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30534460/); [Aarhus RunSafe summary](https://runsafe.au.dk/display/artikel/obese-novice-runners-should-consider-their-start-to-run-distance-carefully)
- **Nielsen group, 1-year cohort, n=930 novice runners with GPS-measured volume.** 254 runners were injured over 155,318 km. BMI >30, age 45–65, noncompetitive behaviour and previous non-running injury were associated with higher injury risk. Grade: direct (BMI as a risk factor under measured volume), observational. — [ClinicalTrials NCT02014987](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02014987); [PMC4555503](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4555503)
- **Nielsen group, n=914, "How do novice runners with different BMIs begin a self-chosen running regime?"** Overweight and obese novices chose roughly the same starting training dose as normal-weight novices. The authors suggest this may partly explain their higher injury risk. Grade: direct (descriptive). — [Aalborg University record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/how-do-novice-runners-with-different-body-mass-indexes-begin-a-se); [PubMed 26535228](https://www.pubmed.gov/26535228/) (I could not open the PubMed record to confirm that this ID matches this paper.)
- Contrasting finding: Vadeboncoeur et al. (Mayo Clinic), a survey of marathon and half-marathon participants, found higher BMI was not associated with greater lower-extremity injury risk. A commentary notes the design cannot say whether heavier runners would be at greater risk at the *same* volume or speed, because heavier runners may self-limit. — [Runblogger summary](https://runblogger.com/2012/05/heavier-runners-less-likely-to-get.html) (secondary source; primary paper not retrieved)

**Partial (BMI/body fat and volume as co-predictors of performance, not stratified):**
- **Tanda & Knechtle 2013, n=126 recreational male marathoners (Basel).** Marathon time depended on mean weekly training distance, mean training pace and body fat %. The body-fat effect appeared only above a threshold. Below it, race time correlated only with training indices. Regression: race time (min) = 11.03 + 98.46·exp(−0.0053·weekly km) + 0.387·training pace (s/km); r = 0.81. — [Dovepress OAJSM](https://www.dovepress.com/marathon-performance-in-relation-to-body-fat-percentage-and-training-i-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAJSM); [PMC3871414](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3871414)
- **Rüst, Knechtle et al. 2011, recreational male half-marathoners.** BMI was the strongest anthropometric correlate and training speed the main training correlate. The combined model explained r² = 0.44. — [Dovepress OAJSM](https://www.dovepress.com/predictor-variables-for-a-half-marathon-race-time-in-recreational-male-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAJSM)
- **Knechtle et al. 2011, 100 km ultra.** Weekly running km and training speed were negatively associated with race time, independently of the sum of skinfolds, which was positively associated with race time. In a 24-h race, anthropometry dropped out and training variables dominated. — [Dovepress OAJSM (marathon/100 km)](https://www.dovepress.com/effects-of-training-and-anthropometric-factors-on-marathon-and-100-km--peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAJSM)
- **Nikolaidis, Rosemann & Knechtle 2021, Athens Marathon, recreational men.** Race speed = 8.804 + 0.111·VO2max + 0.029·weekly km − 0.218·BMI (R² = 0.61). Weekly volume and BMI contribute independently. (Reported via search summary of the Knechtle-group literature; full text not retrieved.) — [search-indexed record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsdoj__edsdoj.999a20c9777a46b0a64c9209e52b2bf1)
- A 2024 narrative review reports that fast marathoners (<3:24) were 4.9 kg lighter and had a 2.5 kg/m² lower BMI than slow marathoners. — [Preprints.org 202407.2249](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202407.2249)

### Inferences
- In recreational racers, weekly volume and training pace appear to predict race time at least as consistently as BMI or body fat. Nothing suggests the volume–performance relationship disappears in heavier runners, but no study has tested a BMI × volume interaction on performance.
- The Danish data imply that for heavy *novices* the limiting factor for volume is tissue tolerance (injury), not aerobic adaptation. Heavier beginners self-select the same dose as lighter beginners, so the same absolute km means a greater relative load.
- The Tanda & Knechtle threshold finding (body fat matters only above a level) hints that in heavier or fatter runners, body composition may explain a larger share of performance variance than in lean runners. This is inference only.

### Gaps
- No study stratifies runners by mass or BMI and examines weekly volume or intensity distribution (e.g. polarized vs threshold) against VO2max, race performance or adherence.
- No study of performance outcomes in the Danish heavy-novice cohorts.
- No RCT of intensity distribution in heavier runners.
- Bertelsen 2018 is the only RCT, n=56, with an injury-only outcome.

## Q2. Is there evidence on "Clydesdale" runners, or heavier/taller athletes (rugby/football converts, heavier masters runners)?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study of "Clydesdale"-category runners was found. Nor were studies found on rugby or American-football players converting to distance running, or on heavier masters runners specifically. The nearest evidence is on large-bodied endurance runners in general (Longman et al. 2022, ultra-runners) and biomechanics of runners with obesity (Vincent et al. 2020).

### Cited Findings
- A targeted search for Clydesdale runners returned no study specific to the category. Results covered only general body-mass-and-marathon literature. — [search results incl. PMC3871414](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3871414)
- **Longman et al. 2022, *Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology*, n=44 ultra-endurance runners (25 M, 19 F).** In men, body mass correlated positively with submaximal VO2 at LT1 (r=0.66, p<0.001) and LT2 (r=0.59, p=0.001), and with VO2max (r=0.60, p=0.001). In women the LT1/LT2 correlations were weak and non-significant (r=0.23), and the VO2max correlation was r=0.41 (p=0.046). The authors conclude that larger runners "compensate for the negative effects their mass has on RE by increasing their capacity to consume oxygen". This is a "low-economy, high-energy-turnover" strategy, with possible costs from energy diverted away from other processes. Grade: partial (heavier runners, no volume/intensity manipulation), cross-sectional, small. — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10142178/1/Wells_Longman2022_Article_AlternativeMetabolicStrategies.pdf); [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Alternative_metabolic_strategies_are_employed_by_endurance_runners_of_different_body_sizes_implications_for_human_evolution/18139658/1)
- **Vincent et al. 2020, *PM&R*, case-control: 18 runners with obesity vs 36 non-obese runners (doi 10.1002/pmrj.12335).** Runners with obesity had 15% less vertical centre-of-mass excursion, 18% wider strides and 3% longer stance. *Normalized* peak GRF and vertical loading rate were **higher in non-obese** runners. Peak hip moments in the sagittal and frontal planes were higher in runners with obesity. The authors conclude that runners with obesity damp impact by raising lower-body stiffness and limiting vertical bounce. — [zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/pmrj.12335); [PMC7801088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7801088)

### Inferences
- Coaching advice for Clydesdale athletes (and for rugby/football converts) is extrapolated from general physiology. No outcome data exist for this group.
- Heavier, already-fit athletes (e.g. ex-team-sport players) likely have high absolute VO2max. The Longman data suggest that absolute aerobic capacity can partly offset the higher absolute cost of moving more mass.

### Gaps
- No Clydesdale cohort studies, race-database analyses of weight-class results, or training studies.
- No studies of team-sport athletes transitioning to endurance running.
- No studies of heavier masters runners specifically.

## Q3. How does body mass affect running economy, metabolic cost, ground reaction forces and thermoregulation, and how does that change the internal load of a given pace/volume?

### Takeaway
The absolute metabolic cost of running rises roughly with body mass, mainly because the body weight must be supported. Expressed per kg, the cost falls slightly as mass rises (allometric exponent ~0.66–0.75). Extra fat mass does not raise absolute VO2max, so relative VO2max (ml/kg/min) falls and a given pace sits at a higher %VO2max. Heavier runners also store more heat in hot conditions. The impact-loading evidence conflicts: absolute forces are higher, but some studies show lower *normalized* loading rates because of gait adaptations. A given pace and volume is therefore a higher internal and mechanical load for a heavier runner. However, no study directly measured HR or %VO2max at matched pace in heavy vs light runners.

### Cited Findings
**Metabolic cost / economy:**
- **Teunissen, Grabowski & Kram 2007, *J Exp Biol* (doi 10.1242/jeb.004481).** Body weight and mass were altered independently. Generating force to support body weight is the primary determinant of the metabolic cost of running. Weight support accounts for at most ~74% of net cost, and the authors say this is probably an overestimate. — [zendy record](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1242/jeb.004481); [catalogue record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_69030688)
- **Bergh, Sjödin, Forsberg & Svedenhag 1991, *MSSE* 23(2):205–211 (doi 10.1249/00005768-199102000-00010).** Running VO2 relates better to body mass raised to ~0.75 or ~0.66 than to kg⁻¹. Reviews therefore recommend allometric scaling, which means per-kg VO2 cost is somewhat *lower* in heavier runners. — [Redalyc review PDF citing Bergh](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3010/301051757003.pdf); [PMC3970734](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3970734); [JHSE allometric scaling paper](https://www.jhse.ua.es/article/view/10849)
- Allometric scaling is not universally accepted. One paper argues the 0.75 exponent may depend on the cohort and methods rather than being a constant. — [UMN experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/allometric-scaling-of-body-mass-in-running-economy-data-an-import/)
- In a torso-loading study (+1.6 to +3.2 kg), metabolic cost rose step-wise with load in men but not clearly in women. The authors say the relationship between changes in mass and running economy is complex and sex-dependent. — [Bohrium record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/33042383)
- Walking (not running) data: obese adults' net metabolic rate per kg during level walking is ~10% higher than in normal-weight adults (Browning et al. 2006), even though external work per kg is similar (Browning, McGowan & Kram 2009). No equivalent running study was found. — [Browning et al. 2009 PDF](https://sites.usc.edu/mcgowanlab/files/2023/02/Browning-et-al-JoB-2009-1.pdf); [PMC2757480](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2757480)

**VO2max and performance with added mass (internal load):**
- **Cureton et al. 1978.** Added weight lowered VO2max relative to total body mass, but not absolute VO2max or VO2max relative to lean mass. It also shortened treadmill endurance time and 12-min run distance. On average each 1% of added weight cost ~0.5 ml/kg/min of relative VO2max and ~18 m of 12-min run distance. — [National Academies Press chapter](https://www.nap.edu/read/2031/chapter/10); [NCBI Bookshelf NBK235957](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK235957); [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/88408faae2feb42fbe3283c0a143fa68)
- **Cureton & Sparling 1980.** Loading men to match paired women's body fat reduced the male–female gap by about one-third for treadmill run time and 12-min run distance, and by two-thirds for VO2max relative to total running weight. — [NAP chapter](https://www.nap.edu/read/2031/chapter/10); [BVS record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/en/mdl-723510)
- Caveat: these are acute added-load experiments, not naturally heavier, adapted runners. — [RunnersConnect (secondary)](https://runnersconnect.net/excess-weight-and-running/)

**Thermoregulation:**
- **Dennis & Noakes 1999, *Eur J Appl Physiol*.** Heat production scales with mass and heat loss with surface area. At 35 °C and 60% RH, a 45 kg runner could maintain thermal balance at ~19.1 km/h, but a 75 kg runner only at ~12.2 km/h. — [record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/48159735)
- **Marino et al. 2000, *Pflügers Archiv* (doi 10.1007/s004240000432).** Heat storage correlated with body mass at 35 °C (r=0.74, p<0.0008), moderately at 25 °C (r=0.50) and not at 15 °C. Lighter runners have a distinct thermal advantage when heat dissipation is at its limit. — [Springer record](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s004240000432)

**Ground reaction forces / joint loading:**
- Vincent et al. 2020: runners with obesity had *lower* normalized peak GRF and loading rate than non-obese runners, with higher hip moments (see Q2). — [PMC7801088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7801088)
- A 2023 *Sports Medicine – Open* study (BMI ≥25 / ≥30) also reported lower vertical impact peak and loading rate in people with excess body weight. It found a body-weight × foot-pronation interaction for medio-lateral loading rate. — [PMC10700293](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10700293)
- In contrast, Piri & Jafarnezhadgero's 2023 systematic review found that two articles reported increased loading rate in obese individuals while running, and that plantar pressure rises with BMI. It concludes that obese and overweight runners face biomechanical injury risk. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/d43060fe24b546738f2c8a6cac263207)
- In healthy-weight adults, peak knee load is ~3× higher in running than walking, but load per unit distance is similar. — [PubMed 24042311](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24042311/)
- A Jyväskylä master's thesis reported knee joint loading rising in proportion to added weight in walking, with a doubled increase in running. (Thesis, not peer-reviewed.) — [JYX PDF](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/46123/1/URN%3aNBN%3afi%3ajyu-201505292105.pdf)
- Obese walkers cut peak sagittal knee moments by ~45% when walking at 1.0 vs 1.5 m/s. Slower walking with a moderate incline is suggested as a way to keep cardiovascular stimulus while reducing knee load. — [JYX thesis PDF / search summary](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/46123/1/URN%3aNBN%3afi%3ajyu-201505292105.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Internal load at a given pace.** Combining Teunissen (cost driven by weight support), Cureton (fat mass doesn't raise absolute VO2max) and Bergh (cost per kg falls only modestly with mass) gives a clear picture. A heavier runner whose extra mass is largely fat runs a given pace at a higher fraction of VO2max, and probably a higher HR, than a lighter runner of similar fitness. The "easy" pace for a lighter runner may be moderate-to-threshold for a heavier one. Prescribing by HR, RPE or %VO2max is therefore more appropriate than prescribing by pace. This is an inference; no study measured it directly in heavy vs light runners at matched speed.
- If the extra mass is largely lean (e.g. ex-rugby players), absolute VO2max tends to scale up (Longman 2022). The relative-intensity penalty would then be smaller, but the absolute mechanical load per step is still larger.
- In heat, heavier runners need lower paces or volumes to stay in thermal balance (Dennis & Noakes; Marino), which adds to internal load in summer training.
- Gait adaptations (stiffer limb, less vertical bounce) may lower *normalized* impact loads, but absolute forces and hip moments rise with mass. This fits the Danish finding that early volume is the injury-sensitive variable.

### Gaps
- No direct comparison of HR, lactate or %VO2max at matched speed between heavy and light trained runners.
- No running-specific study of whether obese adults' net cost per kg differs (the ~10% excess is from walking).
- Conflicting evidence on loading rate in obese runners; studies are small.
- No study of how training volume or intensity *changes* running economy in heavier runners.

## Q4. HIIT vs moderate continuous training in overweight/obese adults: fitness/VO2max outcomes and adherence

### Takeaway
Several meta-analyses show both HIIT and MICT improve VO2max and body composition in overweight and obese adults. HIIT is equal or modestly superior for VO2max, particularly with intervals ≥2 min or when energy expenditure is matched. Body-composition changes are similar, and HIIT takes ~40% less time. Adherence and enjoyment appear similar in small, short trials. These are mostly **non-runner** populations using mixed modes (cycling and treadmill), so they are extrapolation for heavier runners.

### Cited Findings
- **Su et al. 2019, *PLoS One*, meta-analysis, 22 articles, n=620 overweight/obese adults (PMID 30689632).** Both HIIT and MICT reduced weight, BMI, fat % and total cholesterol and improved VO2max. HIIT was superior for cardiorespiratory fitness when intervals were ≥2 min (SMD = 0.444) or when energy expenditure was matched to MICT. — [PMC6349321](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6349321/); [search record 30689632](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/cmedm__30689632)
- **Wewege et al. 2017, *Obesity Reviews*, 13 trials, adults aged 18–45 with overweight/obesity, ~10 weeks, ~3 sessions/week (PMID 28401638).** Both protocols reduced whole-body fat mass and waist circumference, with no significant difference between them on any body-composition measure. HIIT took ~40% less time. **Running** produced significant fat-mass reduction with both protocols, but cycling did not. — [PubMed 28401638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28401638/); [Healio summary](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20170425/highintensity-moderateintensity-interventions-comparable-for-body-composition-improvements)
- **Keating et al. 2017, *Obesity Reviews*, 31 studies (PMID 28513103).** No difference between HIIT/SIT and MICT for any body-fat outcome. Neither short-term approach produced clinically meaningful fat loss. — [QxMD record](https://read.qxmd.com/read/28513103/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-interval-training-versus-moderate-intensity-continuous-training-on-body-adiposity)
- **Türk et al. 2017, *Obesity Science & Practice* 3(3):258–271, 18 RCTs, adults with obesity.** Reviewed whether HIT beats continuous training for cardiopulmonary fitness and body composition. The introduction notes superiority had not been shown in persons with obesity. Pooled VO2max effect not retrieved. — [Wiley doi 10.1002/osp4.109](https://onlinelibrary-wiley-com-443.webvpn.zafu.edu.cn/doi/10.1002/osp4.109)
- **Wen et al. 2019, 53 RCTs (including overweight/obese adults).** VO2max change with HIIT varied by population (SMD 0.41–1.81). — [VU eprint PDF](https://vu-9.eprints-hosting.org/37877/1/PIIS1440244018309198.pdf)
- **Zheng et al. 2025, *Front Physiol* (children/adolescents with obesity, not adults).** HIIT beat MICT for VO2max (SMD 0.81) and SBP (SMD −0.51). Subgroup data were limited. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1625516/full)
- **Adherence: Vella et al. 2017, RCT, n=17 overweight/obese adults (PMID 28792851).** HIIT (10 × 1 min at 75–80% HRR / 1 min at 35–40% HRR) vs MICT. Session adherence was 93.4% vs 93.1%, and mean enjoyment 100.1 vs 100.3, with no difference. In a 5-week independent follow-up (30 min, 4×/week), enjoyment did not change in either group. — [PubMed 28792851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28792851/); [PMC6104631](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6104631)
- A 2022 topical review calls the evidence on HIIT adherence and enjoyment thin and calls for larger, high-quality RCTs. — [PMC8689221](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8689221)
- A 12-week pilot compared treadmill sprint-interval with moderate continuous running in 15 inactive overweight/obese women and measured enjoyment. Results were not retrieved. — [FACETS 2016-0004](https://facetsjournal.com/doi/10.1139/facets-2016-0004)

### Inferences
- For heavier runners chasing fitness gains, HIIT with longer intervals (≥2 min) is at least as effective as continuous running for VO2max in the overweight-adult literature. Because running HIIT means higher-force steps, the injury findings (Q1, Q3) argue for low-impact modes or controlled volume of high-intensity running. This is inference.
- Short-term adherence does not appear to differ between HIIT and MICT. Longer-term, real-world adherence data for running specifically are lacking.

### Gaps
- Few HIIT vs MICT trials use **running** as the mode in overweight adults. None were in trained heavier runners or measured race performance.
- No meta-analysis reports dropout or adherence pooled by BMI.
- Full texts not retrieved (PMC blocked), so modality breakdowns and GRADE ratings were not verified.

## Q5. Evidence on cross-training / low-impact volume substitution for heavy runners

### Takeaway
Evidence is very thin. There are no trials of substituting cross-training (cycling, elliptical, deep-water running, anti-gravity treadmill) for running volume in heavier *runners* with fitness or performance outcomes. Small studies in obese non-runners suggest unweighted treadmill training is tolerable and comparable for weight loss. Deep-water running interval trials exist only as protocols in this search. No elliptical data were found.

### Cited Findings
- **AlterG vs conventional treadmill, 18 weeks, n=15 obese adults (Boise State thesis).** No significant difference in weight loss between groups; both lost weight. — [Boise State ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/td/857)
- **Anti-gravity treadmill pilot, n=26 adults with obesity (17 completers).** Unweighted training significantly improved overall and physical-health quality of life. — [WKU IJES abstract](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol2/iss11/87)
- **Case study, one extremely obese woman on AlterG.** Exercise tolerance time tripled with no added HR. (n=1.) — [Boise State](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/kinesiology_facpubs/18)
- **NCT03690752 (Texas Tech).** Examines whether unweighting improves adherence and fitness in severely obese adults. Listed as completed; no results found. — [Veeva CT record](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/adherence-to-walking-on-an-alter-g-anti-gravity-treadmill)
- **Deep-water running interval training (DWR-IT) RCT protocol, overweight adults.** Outcomes are body composition, functional capacity and QoL. Only the protocol was found. — [PMC6737700](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6737700/)
- Wewege 2017 found running, but not cycling, significantly reduced fat mass in HIIT/MICT trials. This suggests mode substitution may not be equivalent for body-composition goals. — [PubMed 28401638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28401638/)
- In obese walkers, slower speed plus moderate incline reduced knee load while keeping cardiovascular stimulus. This supports incline walking as a low-impact substitute. — [JYX thesis / search summary](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/46123/1/URN%3aNBN%3afi%3ajyu-201505292105.pdf)

### Inferences
- Low-impact modes (incline walking, unweighted treadmill, deep-water running, cycling) are a plausible way to add aerobic volume while limiting the mass-dependent mechanical load. Using them for heavier runners rests on mechanistic reasoning (Q3) and the Danish injury data (Q1), not on outcome trials in runners.
- Specificity matters. Running performance gains from non-running volume are not established in this population.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares running-only with mixed running and cross-training volume in heavier runners on running performance, VO2max, injury or adherence.
- No elliptical data in obese adults were found.
- Published outcomes of the DWR-IT and NCT03690752 trials were not located.
- I did not find a reliable primary source for Weyand & Davis's (2005) "ideal mass per unit speed" figures. They appeared only in a search-summary snippet, so they were left out of the findings.

# Heavier runners train on borrowed evidence

**Almost no research directly answers how much, or how hard, runners over 90 kg should train.** This review found **about four or five studies** that relate running volume to an outcome in heavier or high-BMI runners. Most come from one Danish group (Nielsen and Bertelsen, Aarhus), and the rest from a Luxembourg cohort (Malisoux). **Every one of them measures injury, not performance or fitness.** The review found **no study at all** that relates training *intensity* in heavier runners to performance or fitness, **no study of "Clydesdale" runners**, and **no study that uses a 90 kg cut-off**. Studies use BMI >30, BMI ≥25 or the median body mass instead. What the research does show is that obese novices are injury-sensitive to early volume. In recreational racers, weekly kilometres and BMI predict race time **independently**, so more volume helps heavier runners too, but it does not erase the mass penalty. Physiology implies that a given pace is a **harder internal load** for a heavier runner. For 800 m–10 km runners, total mostly-easy volume tracks performance. Pyramidal, polarized and threshold-focused intensity distributions perform **about equally** on time trials. **No study tests whether body mass changes the performance response to volume or intensity.** For an Australian strength or health coach, the practical guidance is therefore a mix of a few solid injury findings and clearly labelled inference from physiology and general-population trials. One caveat applies throughout: full-text hosts were blocked during the research, so most figures here come from abstracts, registries and search summaries and should be checked before formal use.

## Four injury studies and zero intensity studies make up the direct evidence

The evidence comes in three tiers. The first is **direct** evidence: a heavier or high-BMI running population with volume or intensity measured against an outcome. The second is **partial** evidence: the right population or the right variable, but not both together. The third is **extrapolation** from physiology or from non-runners. The direct tier is small, and all of it concerns novices and injury.

The anchor study is a DANO-RUN analysis of roughly 750–930 Danish novices. The sample size differs between records. It found that runners with **BMI >30 who ran 3–6 km or >6 km in their first week had significantly more injuries** than the low-distance, low-BMI reference group. The authors recommend that obese novices start below **3 km in week 1** ([PubMed 24944852](https://www.pubmed.gov/24944852/)). Bertelsen et al. then ran the only randomised volume trial in heavier runners. They assigned 56 obese novices (BMI 30–35) to start at 3 km or 6 km per week, with both groups adding 10% per week for 4 weeks. The **intention-to-treat result was null**: risk difference −16.3%, 95% CI −43.8% to 11.3%, p=0.25. The per-protocol analysis favoured the lower start, at −31.2% (p=0.02) according to the Aarhus summary ([RunSafe](https://runsafe.au.dk/display/artikel/obese-novice-runners-should-consider-their-start-to-run-distance-carefully); [PubMed 30534460](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30534460/)). Adherence was poor: 15 participants uploaded no data and 5 ran far more than prescribed ([ClinicalTrials NCT02014987](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02014987)). A third Danish paper explains why heavier beginners get hurt. **Overweight and obese novices chose about the same starting dose as normal-weight novices** ([Aalborg University record](https://vbn.aau.dk/da/publications/how-do-novice-runners-with-different-body-mass-indexes-begin-a-se)), so the same kilometres meant a larger relative load. The fourth study, a Luxembourg cohort of 517 recreational runners (Malisoux 2015), found that **BMI modified the relationship between training volume and injury**. Heavier runners in that cohort spread their volume over more, shorter sessions ([LIH research portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/a-step-towards-understanding-the-mechanisms-of-running-related-in/)). The abstract does not make the direction and practical size of the interaction clear.

Around these studies sits a ring of risk-factor evidence. Three independent cohorts point the same way. DANO-RUN found **BMI >30** associated with injury among 930 novices followed for a year over 155,318 km, although the overall test across BMI groups was borderline (P=.06) ([dissem.in](https://dissem.in/p/816182/predictors-of-running-related-injuries-among-930-novice-runners)). In GRONORUN, **25% of overweight novices were injured against 15%** of lighter ones ([BJSM 2011](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/45/4/338.1)). In the Garmin-RUNSAFE cohort, high BMI combined with a running problem in the previous three months marked a high-risk group ([PMC11680559](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11680559/)). The picture changes in established runners. In Malisoux's 848-runner shoe trial, **body mass was unrelated to injury** (SHR 1.00 per kg, 95% CI 0.99–1.01) ([NCT03115437](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03115437)). A Mayo Clinic survey of marathon and half-marathon entrants also found no BMI–injury link, although heavier entrants may simply train less ([Runblogger summary](https://runblogger.com/2012/05/heavier-runners-less-likely-to-get.html)). The fairest reading is that the injury risk from mass is **concentrated in the novice phase**, before tissues adapt. Novices already carry about 2.3 times the hourly injury rate of recreational runners: 17.8 against 7.7 injuries per 1000 h ([Videbæk 2015, PMC4473093](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4473093/)).

| Question | Studies found | Outcome measured | Evidence grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Volume × BMI in heavier runners | ~4–5 (Nielsen 2014, Bertelsen 2018 RCT, Nielsen starting-dose paper, Malisoux 2015) | Injury only | Low–moderate |
| Intensity in heavier runners → performance/fitness | 0 | — | None |
| Clydesdale / >90 kg / team-sport converts | 0 | — | None |
| BMI as injury risk factor (novices) | 3 cohorts agree | Injury | Moderate |
| BMI as injury risk factor (established runners) | Conflicting (Malisoux null, Theisen positive) | Injury | Low |
| Body mass moderating performance response to volume or intensity | 0 | — | None |

## Volume and BMI predict race times as separate levers

No study divides runners by body mass and asks whether more kilometres buy the same performance gain for heavier runners. Several regression studies of recreational racers come close, because they enter weekly volume and BMI side by side. Their consistent message is that **both matter, independently**. In male recreational half-marathoners, predicted race time (min) = 80.056 + 2.498×BMI − 0.594×VO2max − 0.191×weekly km (R²=0.63). That works out to **about 2.5 minutes slower per BMI unit and about 1.9 minutes faster per extra 10 km/week** ([EXCLI J 2023](https://excli.de/excli/article/view/6198)). In Athens Marathon men, race speed rose with VO2max and weekly kilometres and fell with BMI (R²=0.61) ([search-indexed record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsdoj__edsdoj.999a20c9777a46b0a64c9209e52b2bf1)). Among 126 recreational marathoners, Tanda and Knechtle found that weekly distance and training pace explained race time (r=0.81). **Body fat mattered only above a threshold**, and below it training indices alone predicted performance ([Dovepress OAJSM](https://www.dovepress.com/marathon-performance-in-relation-to-body-fat-percentage-and-training-i-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAJSM)). A 2023 study covering 5 km to the marathon linked weekly volume to pace from 5 km to the half marathon, and linked BMI to performance at every distance in both sexes ([Univ. Limerick record](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/anthropometric-training-and-social-variables-associated-with-perf/)).

Two cautions apply to these results. BMI and weekly volume are correlated: heavier runners tend to run less, so the two effects overlap ([EXCLI J 2023](https://excli.de/excli/article/view/6198)). These are also cross-sectional snapshots, and talented or durable runners both tolerate more volume and race faster. Even so, nothing in the data suggests the volume–performance relationship disappears in heavier runners. **Inference:** the Tanda threshold finding hints that for runners carrying substantial fat mass, body composition explains a larger share of performance variance than it does for lean runners. That makes it a second lever alongside training rather than a substitute for it. No study has tested a BMI × volume interaction on performance.

## The same pace costs a heavier runner more

The strongest reason to coach heavier runners differently comes from physiology rather than training studies. Teunissen, Grabowski and Kram showed that **generating force to support body weight is the main determinant of the metabolic cost of running** ([zendy record](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1242/jeb.004481)). Absolute cost therefore rises with mass. Per kilogram, cost falls only modestly, because running VO2 scales with mass to about the 0.66–0.75 power rather than linearly ([Redalyc review citing Bergh 1991](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3010/301051757003.pdf)). The crucial point is what extra *fat* mass does to capacity. In Cureton's added-load experiments, extra weight **left absolute VO2max unchanged but cut relative VO2max by about 0.5 ml/kg/min per 1% of added weight**, and shortened 12-minute run distance by about 18 m per 1% ([National Academies Press](https://www.nap.edu/read/2031/chapter/10)). Lean mass behaves differently. In 44 ultra-runners, heavier men had proportionally higher VO2max (r=0.60) and higher oxygen uptake at both lactate thresholds. Larger runners appear to **compensate for poorer economy with a bigger aerobic engine** ([UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10142178/1/Wells_Longman2022_Article_AlternativeMetabolicStrategies.pdf)).

**Inference, not directly measured:** a heavier runner whose extra mass is mostly fat runs any given pace at a higher fraction of VO2max, and probably a higher heart rate, than a lighter runner of similar fitness. A lighter training partner's "easy" pace can be moderate or even threshold work for the heavier runner. That argues for prescribing intensity by heart rate, RPE or talk test rather than by pace. A heavier athlete with substantial lean mass, such as an ex-rugby or ex-footy player, faces a smaller relative-intensity penalty but still carries a larger mechanical load per step. No study has compared heart rate or lactate at matched speed between heavy and light trained runners.

Heat sharpens this for Australian coaches. Heat production scales with mass, but heat loss scales with surface area. At 35 °C and 60% humidity, Dennis and Noakes calculated that a **45 kg runner could hold thermal balance at about 19 km/h but a 75 kg runner only at about 12 km/h** ([record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/48159735)). Marino et al. found that heat storage correlated with body mass at 35 °C (r=0.74) but not at 15 °C ([Springer record](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/doi/10.1007/s004240000432)). A 100 kg runner falls outside both ranges, which makes summer sessions an even larger internal load for them (inference).

The mechanical picture is more mixed than "heavier means more impact". Runners with obesity adopt a stiffer, flatter gait, with 15% less vertical bounce. Their **body-weight-normalized** peak force and loading rate were *lower* than those of non-obese runners, but their hip moments were higher ([PMC7801088](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7801088)). A systematic review nevertheless reports higher loading rates and plantar pressures with rising BMI ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/d43060fe24b546738f2c8a6cac263207)). Ground-reaction metrics also track tibial bone load poorly ([PLoS ONE 2019](https://doaj.org/article/972aeed0eaad4f7d8b931462091921bc)). Footwear findings go against intuition as well. In Malisoux's RCT, soft cushioning protected runners lighter than the median (SHR 1.80 for hard shoes) but **made no significant difference for heavier runners** (SHR 1.23, 95% CI 0.75–2.03) ([LIH portal](https://researchportal.lih.lu/en/publications/shoe-cushioning-influences-the-running-injury-risk-according-to-b/)).

## Interval training works for heavier adults, but the trials rarely involve running

The intensity evidence for heavier people comes almost entirely from **overweight and obese non-athletes**, often cycling, so it is extrapolation for runners. Within that literature, the results are consistent. A meta-analysis of 22 studies (n=620) found that HIIT and moderate continuous training both improved VO2max and body composition. **HIIT was superior for fitness when intervals lasted ≥2 minutes** (SMD 0.444) or when energy expenditure was matched ([Su 2019, PMC6349321](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6349321/)). Across 13 trials, fat-mass and waist reductions were similar between the two approaches, but HIIT took **about 40% less time**. Running produced significant fat loss under both protocols, whereas cycling did not ([Wewege 2017, PubMed 28401638](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28401638/)). A 31-study review found no clinically meaningful short-term fat loss from either approach ([Keating 2017](https://read.qxmd.com/read/28513103/a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-interval-training-versus-moderate-intensity-continuous-training-on-body-adiposity)). On adherence, a small RCT in overweight adults found **near-identical session adherence (93.4% vs 93.1%) and enjoyment** for HIIT and continuous training ([PubMed 28792851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28792851/)). A 2022 review still calls the adherence evidence thin ([PMC8689221](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8689221)).

Evidence on cross-training as a substitute for running volume is thinner still. Small studies suggest anti-gravity treadmill training is tolerable, with weight loss similar to a conventional treadmill over 18 weeks in 15 obese adults ([Boise State](https://scholarworks.boisestate.edu/td/857)). Deep-water running trials exist only as protocols ([PMC6737700](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6737700/)). **No trial has tested whether swapping running kilometres for low-impact work preserves running performance in heavier runners.**

## 800 m–10 km runners succeed on mostly-easy volume, and the distribution matters less

For the 800 m to 10 km events, descriptive data are rich. World-class 5000/10,000 m runners cover **about 130–190 km/week over 11–14 sessions, with at least 80% at low intensity** ([Haugen 2022, UiT Munin](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/26245)). Volume falls and intensity rises as the race gets shorter. Elite 1500 m runners run 120–170 km/week, and **800 m runners 50–120 km/week**. Their annual split of sessions below versus at or above threshold is about 90/10 and 60/40 respectively. The pairing is inferred from the abstract's event order. 800 m runners also do more lactate-tolerance, strength, power and plyometric work ([Haugen 2021, UiA Brage](https://uia.brage.unit.no/uia-xmlui/handle/11250/2789638?show=full)). Elite distance runners mostly train **pyramidally** (most volume easy, then less at threshold, then least above it) and shift toward polarized near competition. 1500 m runners sit closer to the polarized end ([Casado 2022, PubMed 35418513](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35418513/)).

The volume–performance link is strong in observational data. In 85 mostly Kenyan runners tracked over seven years, **total distance correlated most strongly with performance**, followed by easy runs, tempo runs and short intervals. Long intervals showed only a weak relationship (r≈0.22) ([PubMed 31045681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31045681/)). Small early trials favoured a large easy share. In one, sub-elite runners emphasising low intensity improved a 10.4 km cross-country time by 157 s against 121.5 s for a moderate-emphasis group ([UCM record](https://produccioncientifica.ucm.es/documentos/6038dab085382a6b3d7bb473?lang=en)). The newer pooled evidence flattens the debate about how to distribute intensity. A 17-study meta-analysis found **no time-trial difference between polarized and other distributions** (SMD −0.01, I²=0%). Polarized training led for VO2peak only in highly trained athletes on blocks under 12 weeks ([Oliveira 2024, Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-024-02034-z.pdf)). An individual-participant network meta-analysis found polarized and pyramidal training equivalent. In an exploratory subgroup, **recreational athletes appeared to gain more from pyramidal training** ([Rosenblat 2025, Stirling](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/2095779)). In recreational runners, a threshold-heavy plan matched polarized training on matched load while taking about 17% less time ([Festa 2020, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2019.00070)). In a 60-runner trial, sequencing **pyramidal then polarized** gave the best 5 km gains, about 1.5% ([PubMed 34792817](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34792817/)).

Body mass enters the middle-distance picture mainly through event selection, not through tested interaction. Mass and BMI fall as race distance rises among top international runners ([INSEP HAL](https://hal-insep.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01769542/file/BMI%20a%20performance.pdf)). 800 m runners tend to be taller and heavier than 5–10 km runners ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/234696893.pdf)). Elite 800 m runners range from speed types to endurance types, classified by anaerobic speed reserve ([Sandford 2019, lida record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4054218?lng=en)). The 800 m is roughly two-thirds aerobic, against about 88% for the 1500 m ([Coach Athletics Australia](https://coachathletics.com.au/coaching-education/the-reserve-behind-the-kick)). So the heavier, more powerful physique clusters where training is already lower-volume and speed-and-strength heavy. That is an alignment of event, physique and practice, **not evidence that heavier runners respond differently to the same prescription**. That question remains untested at every level.

### Coaching implications for heavier runners

| Implication | Basis | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Start obese novices at <3 km in week 1, then build gradually | Nielsen 2014 cohort + Bertelsen 2018 RCT (ITT null) | Direct, low–moderate |
| Treat the novice phase as the main injury window; previous injury plus high BMI is the highest-risk combination | DANO-RUN, GRONORUN, Garmin-RUNSAFE | Direct, moderate |
| Cap single runs near the longest run of the past 30 days (spikes >10% raised injury HR 1.5–2.3 in 5,205 runners) | [Frandsen 2025, PMC12421110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12421110) | General runners, moderate; mass modification untested |
| Don't raise pace and volume in the same block | Run Clever secondary analysis (unverified), not BMI-specific | Inference |
| Spread weekly volume over more, shorter sessions | Malisoux 2015 behaviour + BMI × volume interaction | Partial |
| Prescribe intensity by HR, RPE or talk test, not by a lighter partner's pace | Teunissen, Cureton, Bergh physiology | Inference |
| Cut pace or volume in hot conditions more than for lighter athletes | Dennis & Noakes; Marino | Inference from physiology |
| Use pyramidal or threshold-inclusive plans; add ≥2-min intervals for fitness, with impact-controlled volume | Oliveira 2024, Rosenblat 2025, Su 2019 | General runners or overweight non-runners; extrapolated |
| Use incline walking, cycling or an AlterG to add aerobic volume, but keep running as the specific stimulus | Small obese non-runner studies; no runner trials | Inference |
| Don't rely on extra cushioning as injury protection for heavier runners | Malisoux 2020 RCT | Direct, moderate (contradicts intuition) |

## Conclusion

The research gap is itself the most useful finding for a coach. "Heavier runner" programming as it exists today is built from **injury epidemiology in obese novices plus physiology**, not from training trials. Two implications follow. First, the volume limit for a heavy beginner is **tissue tolerance, not aerobic capacity**. The early weeks need conservative dosing, but established heavier runners do not appear to carry much residual injury penalty once adapted. Second, the performance case for heavier runners rests on the same levers as everyone else's: consistent, mostly easy volume with some quality work. Body composition then acts as an additive lever rather than a gate. The real adjustment is how intensity is *measured*, because pace understates the internal cost of carrying more mass, especially in Australian heat.

Several questions remain untested: whether heavier runners need slower progression after the novice phase, whether polarized or pyramidal training suits them differently, and whether lean-heavy ex-team-sport athletes behave more like elite 800 m runners than like obese novices. An evidence base this thin also makes a coach's own monitoring especially valuable. Heart rate at a fixed pace, session RPE and the longest-run cap are cheap to track with heavier clients and would add to what is known.
